# Japanese-language teachers as a target audience for Kanji Kaisen (kanji teaching needs and pain points)

Scope note: research done 2026-10-31. Coverage: ~27 search/fetch calls. Official statistics came from primary PDFs (Japan Foundation, MEXT). Teacher pain-point evidence is thinner: mostly academic papers and resource lists. Reddit/Facebook threads were not surfaced by the search tool (see Gaps).

## How many Japanese teachers, institutions and learners are there (abroad and in Japan)?

### Takeaway
The newest overseas data is the Japan Foundation **FY2024** survey (released Sept 2025). It counts 80,898 teachers, 19,344 institutions and 4,000,750 learners in 143 countries/regions, all record highs. Nearly half the learners are in secondary school, and about 80% are in Asia. Inside Japan (MEXT FY2024, institutions other than K-12 schools), 50,309 "teachers etc." teach 294,198 learners, and **53.1% of those teachers are volunteers**. Public K-12 schools add another 69,123 children who need Japanese-language instruction (2023). The teacher population is small next to the learner market (about 81k abroad plus about 50k in Japan), and it is fragmented.

### Inferences
- Teacher counts are small: about 81k abroad plus about 50k in Japan, some of whom double-count. In English-speaking markets the numbers are tiny: USA about 3.8k and Australia about 3.3k institutional teachers. As a direct user base, teachers are a niche. Their value is as **multipliers**: one teacher can bring 20–150 students, and the largest overseas segment is secondary school (47%).
- The English-language web is the natural first reach, but the biggest teacher populations (China, Korea, Indonesia, Vietnam, Myanmar) are non-English. In China and Korea learners already know kanji/hanja and need less stroke/radical help. Non-kanji-background countries (Indonesia, Australia, USA, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar, India, Sri Lanka, Nepal) are where a radical-story and stroke-order tool fits best.
- Inside Japan the "teacher" is often an unpaid volunteer with no training budget and no institutional software, which favors a free, no-login web tool. These volunteers are numerous (about 27k).

### Gaps
- The teacher breakdown by educational stage for 2024 (and the 2021 stage split for learners/teachers) is only in the JF charts. Text extraction did not capture the values. The full 2024 report (JF index, June 2026 update) should have them.
- The share of native-Japanese teachers abroad was not found in the summary materials.
- No current verified AATJ membership count; no recent US K-12 Japanese enrollment figure (the 2017 ACTFL survey is the latest cited).

## What tools and materials do teachers use for kanji, and what do they complain about?

### Takeaway
Teachers lean on textbook-bundled kanji (e.g. Genki companion sites), flashcard and quiz-game platforms (Quizlet, Kahoot, Gimkit, Quizizz/Wayground), dictionaries with stroke animation (Jisho, Kanji alive), and printed worksheets and tracing sheets. Free kanji-specific worksheet generators are scarce and low quality, and many "stroke-order worksheet" sites are Chinese-focused or SEO spam. That is an open gap.

### Cited Findings
- The Japan Foundation Los Angeles teacher-resource list names Quizlet ("make flashcards, quizzes, and study games"), Kahoot! (interactive quizzes), Gimkit (live game), Mentimeter, Testmoz (no-registration test generator), WaniKani, Memrise, and "Japanese: More than a dictionary" (auto stroke order feature) as tools teachers use. — [JFLA Teaching Resources (Others)](https://www.jflalc.org/jle-teachers-resources-others)
- The Japan Foundation Sydney classroom-resources site provides teacher-made activities such as a "Kakijun relay" stroke-order team game, katakana "memory hint" worksheets that introduce correct stroke order, and Kahoot versions of its quizzes. — [JF Sydney classroom resources](https://classroomresources.sydney.jpf.go.jp/?p=4909); [JF Sydney online lessons survival kit](https://classroomresources.sydney.jpf.go.jp/resources/online-lessons-survival-kit/)
- A Genki companion site (CSU Sacramento) offers kanji stroke-order MP4 videos, drag-and-drop exercises, worksheets and flashcards. Teachers who use one textbook want resources mapped to its lessons. — [CSUS Genki site](https://www.csus.edu/faculty/m/masuyama/genki/)
- The AP Japanese (College Board) classroom-resources page includes an article on teaching kanji effectively and a list of online resources recommended by AP teachers. A University of Wisconsin K-12 list includes Kanji alive and Jisho as free tools. — [AP Central](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-japanese-language-and-culture/classroom-resources); [UW–Madison East Asia K-12 resources](https://eastasia.wisc.edu/k-12-educator-resources/language-study-resources/)
- Quizizz (now Wayground) and Tes host teacher-made kanji flashcard sets and resources by school year (e.g. "kanji flashcards year 10"). This points to a market for ready-made, curriculum-sized sets. — [Quizizz kanji flashcards](https://quizizz.com/en-gb/kanji-flashcards-year-10); [Tes Japanese resources](https://www.tes.com/en-au/teaching-resources/hub/secondary/languages/japanese/language-and-linguistics)
- Kahoot! in Japanese class: an Indonesian high-school study examined teacher implementation, student perceptions and constraints. A Riau University study found Kahoot effective as kanji learning media. — [Journal of Education Technology (Undiksha)](https://doi.org/10.23887/jet.v6i2.46102); [JOM FKIP Unri](https://jom.unri.ac.id/index.php/JOMFKIP/article/view/35143)
- Worksheet generators: a web search for kanji worksheet generators surfaced mostly Chinese hanzi generators (an2.net), kana-only generators (Gyanmirai), a promotional AI page (OpenQuiz: JLPT N5–N1 sets) and many cloned SEO-spam pages. No well-established teacher-oriented kanji generator appeared. — [an2.net](https://www.an2.net/en); [Gyanmirai hiragana generator](https://www.gyanmirai.com/tools/hiragana-worksheet-generator); [OpenQuiz kanji printable](https://openquiz.ai/learning-guides/free-kanji-writing-practice-printable.html)
- Forum complaint: users said some free kanji practice sheets lacked stroke-order diagrams. Another thread notes that high-school kanji test PDFs are incomplete. — [JapanesePod101 forum](https://www.japanesepod101.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=43165); [japaneseclass.jp forum](https://japaneseclass.jp/forum/thread/1512)
- A report on Google Classroom's handwriting input for kanji found good learner reactions but practical problems: slowness, inaccurate character recognition, and no printing. (Search-result summary of a J-STAGE/university repository paper; exact paper not verified.) — [J-STAGE JLEM](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jlem/30/2/30_8/_article/-char/ja)

### Inferences
- The tools teachers use are general-purpose quiz platforms with kanji content typed in by teachers, plus static PDFs. Nothing combines **a projectable stroke-order animation, radical decomposition and printable sheets** in one place keyed to Genki, Minna no Nihongo or Marugoto lesson lists. Textbook-mapped "class sets" plus print output is the most defensible wedge.
- Quizlet/Kahoot are entrenched for review games. Kanji Kaisen should integrate (e.g. export a set to Quizlet/Kahoot CSV) rather than compete on games.

### Gaps
- No systematic survey of which kanji tools teachers use, or how often, was found.
- Teacher-specific complaints about Quizlet (paywalls such as Learn mode going paid) or Anki in classrooms were not verified in this round.
- Kanji alive's teacher usage and any feedback were not found.

## What are teachers' pain points (worksheets, stroke-order demos, mixed levels, motivation, handwriting assessment, time, device/privacy, budget)?

### Takeaway
The best-documented pain points are **limited class time** (kanji gets squeezed into 5–10 minutes or left to rote homework), **difficulty for non-kanji-background learners** (form memorization, stroke order, rising load of readings), **uncertainty about sequencing and method**, and **handwriting feedback that digital tools do poorly**. Privacy and procurement rules (COPPA/FERPA, district approval lists) push K-12 teachers toward tools that need no student accounts.

### Cited Findings
- Time: one analysis describes typical instruction as a few kanji introduced per day, stroke order practiced as the instructor writes in the air or uses flashcards, and occasional quizzes. The process is generally limited to the start of class, "most likely 5 to 10 minutes." (Princeton-hosted document, as summarized in search results; the direct fetch returned 403.) — [Princeton PJPF document](https://pjpf.princeton.edu/document/69)
- Time and rote learning: a Japan Foundation book-review column says that in practice, with limited time, kanji study tends to be framed as memorization and left to learners. Teachers ask for ways to make it enjoyable. — [JF 日本語教育通信 本ばこ: 『日本語教師のための 実践・漢字指導』](https://www.jpf.go.jp/j/project/japanese/teach/tsushin/bookshelf/201012.html)
- Non-kanji learners: in early stages, non-kanji-background learners struggle with acquiring character forms and linking sound, form and meaning. As vocabulary grows, readings and stroke orders become more complex and the burden rises. — [Tsukuba repository paper](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/6205/files/4.pdf); [Waseda repository](https://waseda.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/24472/files/35080_a.pdf)
- Method: kanji form teaching has traditionally relied on stroke-order teaching alone. Research proposes "structure teaching" (teaching hen/tsukuri components) alongside stroke order. This directly supports a radical/component approach. — [J-STAGE JLEM 25(2)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jlem/25/2/25_88/_article/-char/ja)
- Sequencing: a Macquarie PhD thesis (Paxton, 2022) surveyed Japanese teachers at universities in Australia, NZ, Canada, the UK and the US. A large proportion believe the order in which kanji are introduced matters, but they are unsure how to act on it in class. — [Paxton thesis, Macquarie](https://figshare.mq.edu.au/articles/thesis/Tackling_the_kanji_hurdle_an_investigation_of_kanji_order_and_its_role_in_facilitating_the_kanji_learning_process/19441424)
- Learner difficulty (teacher-side burden): Hanoi University first-year majors rated kanji the hardest component. The main difficulties were memorizing forms and stroke orders. — [HANU journal](https://journals.hanu.edu.vn/index.php/tckhnn/article/view/743)
- Non-native teachers: a questionnaire of 60 non-native Japanese teachers from non-kanji backgrounds examined their own kanji learning. Many overseas teachers are themselves non-native and have struggled with kanji. — [Hamakawa, CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390001205781047296)
- Some programs drop kanji: a study of Kenyan tertiary programs found some teachers do not consider kanji necessary, and some institutions do not teach it. — [Kenyatta University repository](https://ir-library.ku.ac.ke/items/acbcaf81-38d6-4613-839d-7cf6633af99f)
- Handwriting feedback: an Australian curriculum lesson plan suggests projecting animated stroke sequences, then having students trace faint worksheets before freehand writing. Teachers note stroke-order errors and give targeted feedback the next lesson, which is manual and delayed. — [Kuraplan basic kanji skills plan](https://www.kuraplan.com/au/plans/basic-kanji-skills-066afa7e)
- Privacy/procurement (US K-12, general, not Japanese-specific): under COPPA, students under 13 can't create their own accounts, so teachers have to make accounts for them. — [Common Sense Education: What is COPPA](https://www.commonsense.org/index%2Ephp/education/articles/what-is-coppa). A 2020 survey found 51% of edtech-using teachers needed parental consent, 39% needed administrative approval, and 52% said their school had a pre-approved edtech list. — [Program on Economics & Privacy / Mason LEC Edtech Report (2020)](https://masonlec.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Edtech-Report.pdf). CoSN's 2025 report (400 district edtech leaders, fieldwork Jun–Sep 2024): nearly 90% oversee student data privacy, but 75% say it isn't in their job description. — [CoSN 2025 National Student Data Privacy Report](https://www.cosn.org/tools-and-resources/resource/2025-national-student-data-privacy-report-part-one)
- Budget: AATJ reports financial pressure leading schools to reduce or phase out Japanese programs. — [AATJ Advocacy S.O.S. 2021](https://www.aatj.org/wp/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/AATJ-Advocacy-S.O.S.-Introduction-August-2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- These pain points map onto Kanji Kaisen's concepts:
  - Projector mode with animated stroke order covers the "write in the air" 5–10 minute slot.
  - Radical stories match the "structure teaching" research and the need to make kanji "enjoyable".
  - Class sets mapped to textbooks address sequencing uncertainty and save prep time.
  - Printable worksheets cover tracing and practice, and work where devices are banned or unavailable.
  - Shareable links with no student login avoid COPPA, GDPR and district-approval friction.
- A strong selling point for K-12 is "no student accounts, no personal data collected". It should be stated explicitly on a teacher page.
- Handwriting assessment is a real need, but stroke recognition is hard; even Google Classroom handwriting input disappointed. Better to support print-and-mark workflows (answer-key sheets, stroke-numbered models) than to promise automatic grading.

### Gaps
- No quantitative survey of Japanese-language teachers' kanji pain points (e.g. % citing time or materials) was found. JF surveys ask about satisfaction with materials and teachers, but those results weren't extracted.
- Mixed-level classes and motivation are widely assumed but were not documented with a kanji-specific source in this round.
- GDPR/EU school restrictions specific to language-teaching tools were not researched.

## Do Japanese-language education for foreign residents in Japan (地域日本語教育) and heritage/kids learners (継承語) have distinct needs?

### Takeaway
Yes. Community classes are majority-volunteer, use few kanji-specific materials, and focus on 生活 (daily-life) kanji: signs, forms, school letters. Heritage learners hit a "kanji wall" around grades 3–4 at Saturday hoshū schools, where classes run several times faster than weekday school and parents (often volunteers) carry home review. Children in Japanese public schools (69k) need kanji tied to school subjects (教科) and the 学年別漢字配当表.

### Cited Findings
- 53.1% of the 50,309 Japanese "teachers etc." in Japan are volunteers. In local government, international exchange association and voluntary group classes, roughly 85–95% are volunteers. — [MEXT FY2024 report](https://www.mext.go.jp/content/20251114-mxt_nihongo01-000045594_2.pdf)
- Researchers frame the question as whether "kanji" is needed in community Japanese classes. They start from the premise that living in Japan while avoiding kanji is nearly impossible, and examine volunteers' and learners' attitudes to kanji via volunteer interviews. — [Ochanomizu Univ. presentation abstract (Fukushima & Hamakawa)](https://teapot.lib.ocha.ac.jp/record/38665/files/25_141-144.pdf)
- A Kanagawa academy resource asks whether teaching straight from a general comprehensive textbook works for community learners, which suggests a need for life-situation materials. — [Kanagawa Prefecture document](https://www.pref.kanagawa.jp/documents/6016/570839.pdf)
- Local material development exists: Shimane University developed a Matsue-region daily-life Japanese booklet with local partners. — [Shimane Univ. repository](https://ir.lib.shimane-u.ac.jp/6191/files/1029)
- Volunteer support: the Agency for Cultural Affairs lists practical training courses for community volunteers by prefecture. A 2015 Aichi survey of 397 volunteers aimed to build an operations handbook. — [Bunka-cho volunteer training list](https://www.bunka.go.jp/seisaku/kokugo_nihongo/kyoiku/seikatsusha/h22_volunteer/index.html); [Aichi Prefecture report](https://www.pref.aichi.jp/uploaded/attachment/288750.pdf)
- Heritage learners: at hoshū (supplementary) schools, children learning Japanese as a heritage language hit a wall in grades 3–4, when new kanji and readings increase. Some quit at that point. The researcher argues for kanji instruction adapted to children raised in English environments. — [Trinity College abstract (McKissock, 2023)](https://www.trincoll.edu/language-culture-studies/wp-content/uploads/sites/21/2023/04/MihoMcKissock_abstract.pdf)
- Hoshū schools move several times faster than weekday Japanese schools and cannot cover the whole textbook in class, so drill homework does the review. Parents report not knowing what children study. — [Japanese School Zurich newsletter (Dec 2025)](https://jszurich.ch/js200_hoshu/hodayori/2025/hosyuukoudayori20251220.pdf)
- Some heritage schools are run entirely by parent volunteers who teach their own child's grade (Taipei example). — [Osaka Univ. repository (MHB journal)](https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/102025/mhb_020_sp_120.pdf)
- KAKEN research on parent-led heritage groups documents their education activities and the challenges parents face. — [KAKEN 18K12435](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/ja/report/KAKENHI-PROJECT-18K12435/18K12435seika)
- In Japanese public schools, 69,123 children need Japanese instruction (2023). The largest home languages are Portuguese and Chinese, and among Japanese nationals, Filipino. — [MEXT press release Aug 2024](https://www.mext.go.jp/content/20240808-mxt_kyokoku-000037366_01.pdf)

### Inferences
- Distinct needs by segment:
  1. **Community volunteers** need ready-to-use, free, untrained-friendly materials around daily-life kanji (station signs, garbage-sorting notices, forms, medicine labels), plus explanations in learners' L1 (Portuguese, Vietnamese, Nepali, Filipino, Indonesian, Burmese).
  2. **Heritage/hoshū teachers and parents** need materials aligned to the Japanese school grade-kanji list (学年別漢字配当表) and to 国語 textbooks rather than JLPT. They also need parent-facing explanations in English or other languages, and home-practice printables.
  3. **Public-school JSL (日本語指導) teachers** need grade-list kanji plus subject vocabulary, for children whose home language is Portuguese, Chinese, Filipino, etc.
- Kanji Kaisen's JLPT/textbook framing would not serve heritage kids unless a grade-level (小1–小6) atlas view is added. This could be a low-cost differentiator, since the data is public.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on what kanji community-class learners most need (no list of "life kanji" priorities was verified).
- Number of hoshū schools and heritage learners worldwide was not retrieved (MOFA/JOES publishes counts).
- No data on device availability in community classes.

## Are there online teacher communities, and what do they ask for?

### Takeaway
Structured communities exist: AATJ in the US (1,000+ members), Japan Foundation regional teacher hubs (Sydney, LA) with resource-sharing sites, the AP Japanese teacher network, and Japanese-side 日本語教師 networks and publishers' seminars. What teachers ask for shows mainly in what these hubs publish: ready-made games, textbook-aligned worksheets, and online-lesson kits. Search did not surface Facebook groups or Reddit threads, so that evidence is missing.

### Cited Findings
- AATJ: a US organization of more than 1,000 teachers. It runs advocacy webinars (e.g. Sept 2024) focused on program survival. — [Georgetown](https://college.georgetown.edu/?p=24605); [AATJ advocacy webinar 2024](https://www.aatj.org/wp/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/AATJ_-Webinar_-Advocacy-9_28_2024_Compressed.pdf); [AATJ advocacy page](https://aatj.org/advocacy)

It also ran the 2024 survey outreach to Australian teachers. — [JF Sydney classroom resources](https://classroomresources.sydney.jpf.go.jp/resources/online-lessons-survival-kit/); [JF Sydney 2024 survey message](https://sydney.jpf.go.jp/language/for-teachers/2024-survey-of-japanese-language-education/message-from-the-japan-foundation/)
- Japan Foundation Los Angeles publishes teacher-resource lists and JLE reports for US teachers. — [JFLA teacher resources](https://www.jflalc.org/jle-teachers-resources-others); [JFLA JLE reports](https://www.jflalc.org/jle-reports)
