# Global Japanese-Learner Market: Size, Demographics, Motivations, and Social-Media Presence (as of Oct 2026)

Note on sources: the Japan Foundation (JF) 2024 survey figures below were extracted directly from the official English PDF "Overview of Survey Results" (PDF created 22 May 2026). JLPT figures were taken directly from jlpt.jp official statistics pages and the official per-test-site PDF. Derived totals (annual sums, shares, growth rates) are my own arithmetic on those official tables and are marked "(calc)".

## 1. Learner numbers by country, growth trends, JLPT applicants, Duolingo ranking

### Takeaway
Institutional learners hit a record 4,000,750 in 2024 (+5.4% vs 2021). JLPT applications grew much faster, reaching ~1.94M in 2025 (+12.9% vs 2024, about +31% vs 2023). The number of test-takers is growing faster than classroom enrolment, which suggests that self-study and exam-driven learners (the natural market for a web tool) are the expanding segment. N3 and N2 are now the biggest JLPT levels; N5 is small and almost entirely overseas.

### Inferences
- There are three tiers of market. (a) Learners at institutions: ~4.0M (2024). (b) Exam-takers: ~1.65M actual sitters and 1.94M registrations in 2025, overlapping heavily with (a) and with foreign residents of Japan. (c) A much larger self-study and app population that JF excludes. Duolingo's ranking of Japanese at #4 implies this group is several times larger than (a). Duolingo does not disclose per-language MAU, so I cannot size it.
- Applications grew +12.9% in 2025 against +5.4% growth over three years for institutional learners. Demand for certification is outpacing enrolment, which points to exam-oriented self-study tools.
- N3 and N2 are the fastest-growing and largest levels; N1 is shrinking. The kanji-load "sweet spot" for a tool is the N4→N2 range (roughly 300 to 1,000 kanji). N5 alone is a small market outside beginners in South Asia.
- The +23% growth in Dec 2025 applicants inside Japan probably reflects foreign residents and workers (see §3). It is a sizeable segment that is already in Japan and motivated by work.

### Gaps
- Duolingo per-language learner counts and Japanese-specific growth percentages are not published.
- I did not check the official JLPT 2023 annual by-level table; the 2023 figure (~1.48M) comes from the statistics index snippet.
- I did not extract country-level JLPT figures for July 2025; only the Dec 2025 PDF was parsed.
- No authoritative estimate of the total number of self-learners worldwide exists.
- The Chinese-education-blog claim of ~182k Chinese applicants in July 2025 is unverified — [XDF blog](https://liuxue.xdf.cn/blog/blog_7663838.shtml)

## 2. Motivations, age distribution, self-study vs institutional share

### Takeaway
Pop culture (anime, manga, J-POP, fashion) is the top reason for learning Japanese in 2024, cited by 61.3% of institutions. It is followed by interest in the language itself (56.8%) and history/arts (44.4%). Motivations tied to Japan rose after COVID: study in Japan 37.3%, work in Japan 36.4% and tourism 33.9%. Secondary school is the largest age band (47.4% of learners). Non-school learning grew fastest (+49%) and now exceeds higher education.

### Cited Findings
- Learning objectives across all stages in 2024 (multiple choice; % of responding institutions, not of individual learners; n=19,344 institutions), with 2021 values in brackets — [JF overview PDF, Graph 1-5-1](https://www.jpf.go.jp/e/project/japanese/survey/result/information/dl/overview_of_survey_results_en.pdf):
  - Anime, manga, J-POP, fashion etc.: 61.3% [59.9%]
  - Interest in the Japanese language: 56.8% [60.1%]
  - History, literature, arts: 44.4% [47.9%]
  - Study in Japan: 37.3% [34.2%]
  - Future work in Japan: 36.4% [34.5%]
  - Sightseeing in Japan: 33.9%
  - Advancement in home-country education: 29.8% [35.6%]
  - Work in home country: 24.9% [40.6%]
  - Science/technology: 21.4% [23.3%]
  - Cross-cultural understanding: 20.1% [24.0%]
  - Politics/economy/society: 13.4% [18.0%]
  - Heritage language: 11.0% [9.8%]
- Motivations by stage in 2024 — [JF overview PDF, Graphs 1-5-2 to 1-5-5](https://www.jpf.go.jp/e/project/japanese/survey/result/information/dl/overview_of_survey_results_en.pdf):
  - Secondary: anime etc. 65.8%; language 58.3%; work in Japan 27.2%
  - Higher education: anime etc. 69.0%; language 62.3%; history/arts 59.5%; study in Japan 49.2%; work in Japan 43.8%
  - Non-school (language schools, private courses): anime etc. 54.8%; work in Japan 52.8% (highest of any stage); study in Japan 50.1%
- Learners by educational stage in 2024 — [JF overview PDF, Graph 1-3-1](https://www.jpf.go.jp/e/project/japanese/survey/result/information/dl/overview_of_survey_results_en.pdf):
  - Primary: 305,822 (7.6%, −7.8%)
  - Secondary: 1,896,652 (47.4%, +7.7%)
  - Higher: 889,014 (22.2%, −8.4%)
  - Non-school: 909,262 (22.7%, +49.0%). This is the first time non-school learners outnumbered higher-education learners.
- Online class availability by stage: non-school 56.2%, higher education 42.7% — [JF overview PDF](https://www.jpf.go.jp/e/project/japanese/survey/result/information/dl/overview_of_survey_results_en.pdf)
- In 2024, 32% of Duolingo Japanese learners in China were learning "just for fun" — [Duolingo 2024 report](https://blog.duolingo.com/2024-duolingo-language-report/)
- In a Duolingo survey of more than 4,700 learners in Japan, ~58% used language apps, against 37% for video platforms and 36% for textbooks. This concerns Japanese people learning other languages, so it is only indirectly relevant — [MarketScreener/Kyodo](https://marketscreener.com/quote/stock/DUOLINGO-INC-125228573/news/Japan-Duolingo-app-users-spend-longest-time-on-language-study-report-42646822)

### Inferences
- There are two distinct target personas with different UX needs:
  - "Culture-first" learners: teens and university students in East and Southeast Asia, Oceania, the Americas and Europe. They are motivated by anime and the language itself, and respond to fun, curiosity, aesthetics and streaks.
  - "Career-first" learners: non-school learners in Myanmar, Vietnam, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, India and Indonesia. They are motivated by work or study in Japan and are exam-driven (N4 for SSW visas, N3/N2 for jobs). They respond to JLPT-aligned progress and efficiency.
- Secondary-school students make up nearly half of institutional learners. A web tool that runs on school Chromebooks or phones without installation suits this group.

### Gaps
- JF records motivations per institution, not per learner. I found no learner-level percentage survey.
- JF does not give age in years, only educational stage. I found no reliable global age distribution of self-learners.
- The global self-study share is unknown, because JF excludes self-learners by design.

## 3. Fastest-growing countries and the UI languages they need

### Takeaway
The fastest growth is in South and Southeast Asia, driven by labour migration. Learner growth 2021→2024: Myanmar +425%, Sri Lanka +256%, Nepal +162%, India +47%, Brazil +29%, Korea +18%. Over the same period the US (−17%), Taiwan (−14%), China (−4%), Vietnam (−3%) and the Philippines (−4%) fell at institutions, although work-visa flows from Vietnam, Indonesia, Nepal and Myanmar keep rising. Duolingo's experience suggests native-language UI is itself a growth lever.

### Cited Findings
- Fastest institutional growth 2021→2024: Myanmar +424.6%, Sri Lanka +255.5%, Nepal +162.2%, India +47.0%, Brazil +28.8%, Korea +18.1%. South Asia as a region +104.3%. The largest increases came from Korea (+85,062), Myanmar (+81,191), Sri Lanka, Indonesia and India. The largest decreases were in China, the US and Taiwan — [JF overview PDF](https://www.jpf.go.jp/e/project/japanese/survey/result/information/dl/overview_of_survey_results_en.pdf)
- Foreign workers in Japan reached a record 2,571,037 at end-October 2025 (+11.7% YoY, the 13th straight record) — [Tuoi Tre](https://news.tuoitre.vn/foreign-workers-in-japan-hit-record-257-million-103260130174306871.htm); [nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/news/yjj2026013000531). By nationality:
  - Vietnam ~606k (+6.2%)
  - China ~432k
  - Philippines ~261k
  - Nepal 235,874 (+25.7%)
  - Indonesia 228,118 (+34.6%)
  - Myanmar +42.5% (fastest growth; headcount not found)

  Sources differ slightly on rankings and rounding. One gives Vietnam ≈610k — [nippon.com Japan Data](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02693/)
- In Dec 2025, beginner-level JLPT demand was heavy in South Asia: Bangladesh had 12,350 of 17,059 applicants at N5, and India 10,404 of 21,929. Myanmar's applicants clustered at N4/N3, the levels that match visa and job thresholds — [JLPT 2025_2_3.pdf](https://www.jlpt.jp/statistics/pdf/2025_2_3.pdf)
- Duolingo credits Japanese's rise to #4 in 2025 to making Japanese courses available to speakers of more than 20 languages — [Duolingo 2025 report](https://blog.duolingo.com/2025-duolingo-language-report/)
- In 2024, Japanese was the #1 language on Duolingo in the Philippines — [Duolingo 2024 report](https://blog.duolingo.com/2024-duolingo-language-report/)
- Chinese-speaking learners have a recognised kanji advantage, but are hindered by different readings and usages. Chinese commentary describes kanji as the main head start and grammar and word order as the main hurdle — [XDF](https://xyz.xdf.cn/beijing/riyu/study/202008/11091560.html); [Zhihu](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/137218353) (older, non-academic sources)

### Inferences
- Suggested UI-language priority for a kanji tool, by audience size and growth:
  1. English: a lingua franca for India, Philippines, Australia, US/UK, and much of Europe and South Asia.
  2. Simplified Chinese: China, with ~1.02M institutional learners and the largest JLPT pool at ~175k per sitting. These learners need a "kanji ≠ hanzi" angle (readings, shinjitai vs simplified forms, false friends), not stroke-from-zero teaching.
  3. Indonesian: 733k learners and fast worker growth.
  4. Korean: 555k learners, +18%.
  5. Vietnamese: the largest worker group in Japan, plus Sino-Vietnamese (Hán-Việt) readings as a hook.
  6. Traditional Chinese: Taiwan and Hong Kong.
  7. Thai.
  8. Burmese: Myanmar is the fastest-growing country, strongly N4/N3.
  9. Spanish and Portuguese: Brazil, Latin America, Spain; Duolingo's new course pairs show demand.
  10. Later: Nepali, Sinhala, Bengali, Hindi.
- Non-kanji-background learners (Myanmar, South Asia, SE Asia ex-Vietnam, the West) find kanji hardest. They need radicals, mnemonics and visuals. Chinese, Taiwanese and Hong Kong learners need readings and vocabulary differences instead.

### Gaps
- I found no published breakdown of Japanese learners by native/UI language on any app.
- Myanmar's absolute foreign-worker headcount (Oct 2025) was not found.
- Vietnam's institutional decline of −3% conflicts with its rising worker numbers. JF does not explain this; it may reflect a shift to private, non-surveyed training.

## 4. Social media: creators, formats, engagement, and aesthetic/visual content

### Takeaway
Japanese learning is a large hashtag ecosystem on TikTok (#learningjapanese reports ~245M viewers). It is led by personality-driven native-speaker teachers and by "Japanese is absurd" humour hooks, often built around kanji or counting quirks. Aesthetic study content (studygram, Lemon8, techo/journaling) exists as a visual sub-genre, but I found no hard engagement data for it. I also found no reliable metrics for Japanese-learning content on Xiaohongshu or Bilibili, so those platforms are a research gap.

### Cited Findings
- TikTok: the #learningjapanese tag page reports "244.7M people have watched this" (scraped discover page; date unclear) — [TikTok tag](https://www.tiktok.com/amp/tag/learningjapanese?lang=en)
- TikTok creator Real Real Japan uses kanji/number-quirk humour hooks. "They just had to make it as confusing as possible #japaneseistoohard" got ~113K likes and 623 comments — [TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@realrealjapanese/video/7452740244039814430). "千チ…ok it's a bit different" got ~96.9K likes and 653 comments — [TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@realrealjapanese/video/7469089050666781983) (both via search snippet; counts as scraped)

- Nihongo de Kita (Sayaka; native teacher) has 1.51M YouTube, 785,667 Instagram and 860,600 TikTok followers (3.2M combined), with a 5.5% engagement rate (data 8 Oct 2026) — [CreatorDB](https://creatordb.app/creatorstats/nihongodekita/)

- Aesthetic/visual study content (anecdotal):
  - Lemon8 and Instagram "studygram" posts pair kanji and hiragana drills with pastel or cozy layouts and colour-coded notes (e.g., different pens for particles and conjugations) — [Lemon8 @eternal.tay](https://www.lemon8-app.com/@eternal.tay/7447635861312913966?region=ca); [Lemon8 @jahnipop](https://www.lemon8-app.com/@jahnipop/7280978800002040326?region=ca)

- Bilibili: knowledge content ("泛知识") has grown strongly since H2 2019. ### Inferences
- Hooks that appear to work:
  1. "Japanese is absurd/too hard" humour, often about kanji readings or counters.
  2. A native-speaker personality teaching one point per video.
  3. Anime-scene breakdowns.
  4. Study-method and "how I learn" content.
  5. Pastel aesthetic notes and calendars on Instagram, Lemon8 and Pinterest.

  Kanji etymology shorts ("this kanji is a picture of…") fit hooks 1 and 5 and are under-served by large channels, so there is white space for a kanji tool to create its own shareable visual content.
- For Chinese-language platforms (Xiaohongshu, Bilibili, Douyin), the likely hook is the Chinese learner's kanji advantage turned into a trap ("这些日语汉字和中文意思完全不同", i.e., "these Japanese kanji mean something completely different from Chinese"). This is my inference from the kanji-advantage and interference discourse; I have no metrics for it.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable view or engagement numbers for "kanji etymology" or "kanji you can guess" shorts. TikTok and Instagram pages did not render for fetching.
- I found no data on Japanese-learning creators, follower counts or note performance on Xiaohongshu or Bilibili. Paid tools (Qiangua, Feigua, Newrank) or in-app searches would be needed.
- I could not verify X (Twitter) Japanese-learning trends.
- No A/B or platform data compares aesthetic content with plain instructional content.

## 5. Monetization norms: willingness to pay and price points

### Takeaway
Paid Japanese study tools sit around US$5–15/month, $50–96/year, or $150–300 lifetime. Kanji-specific SRS (WaniKani) commands $9/month, $89/year or $299 lifetime, with periodic lifetime sales near $200. Lifetime plans and seasonal sales are a strong norm in this community. I found no hard ARPU or willingness-to-pay survey data specific to Japanese learners.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- A kanji web tool would sit at the ~$5–9/month or ~$50–90/year range, below WaniKani, with a lifetime option around $100–200. That range matches community norms. Exam (JLPT-level) packaging is the clearest willingness-to-pay trigger.
- The fastest-growing segments (Myanmar, Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, India, Indonesia, Vietnam) have much lower purchasing power. Regional pricing or a generous free tier would be needed there, with monetization concentrated in the US, EU, Australia, Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore. This is inference only; no price-sensitivity data was found.
