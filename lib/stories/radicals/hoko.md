## Origin

The hoko radical (戈, 4 strokes) is a pictograph of the ancient Chinese dagger-axe: a long wooden shaft with a blade fixed crosswise near the top. It was one of the main weapons of early Chinese armies. On the right side of a kanji it is called hokozukuri. Don't confuse it with 矛, a different radical that is also read "hoko" and shows a thrusting spear.

## Story

Arm yourself! The long slanting stroke is the shaft, the short horizontal stroke is the blade, and the little dot is the tassel tied near the top. This radical brings the energy of soldiers, battle, and standing guard. When you see it, think of weapons, conflict, and the strength to defend what matters.

## Position

Usually appears on the RIGHT side of the kanji (hokozukuri), sometimes wrapping around the right and top as in 成.

## Transforms Meaning

Indicates weapons, war and fighting, guarding and defense, or the use of force.

## Example Kanji

### 戦 (war / battle)

Single/simple (単) + halberd (戈). Here 単 is mainly the sound element, while the halberd says it all: a battle, as in 戦争 (war).

### 戒 (admonish / guard against)

Halberd (戈) + two hands (廾). Two hands gripping a weapon, ready and on guard—to be cautious or to warn, as in 警戒 (vigilance).

### 我 (I / self)

Originally a picture of a weapon with a jagged, saw-toothed blade. Its sound was borrowed to write the word for "I, oneself," as in 自我 (the self).
